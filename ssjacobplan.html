Bonus on top

When a job gets done well and gets done efficiently, there's a bonus on it. It scales with the job — the bigger the job, the bigger the bonus.

I'm not putting a number on it here, because it depends on how the job actually goes. What I will tell you is what earns it: the work is clean, the customer is happy enough to put our name in front of a neighbor, and we're off the property without burning the whole day. That part is mostly in your hands, which is the point of it.

What changes

The shape of your pay changes more than anything else. Right now you're riding on how well I price a job. The good ones are good — a $2,000 job we knock out in two days paid you $1,400. But the ones that run long, the ones I underprice and the days that go sideways come out of your end too.

Hourly evens that out. That same two-day job comes to $790, so the quick well-priced ones won't pay like they used to. In exchange, a job that takes four days instead of two pays you for four days, and one I price badly stops being your problem.

You get paid for every hour you work, on a schedule, whether or not the job went to plan. Where it lands over a year comes down to how much work I can keep in front of you — which is the thing I'm restructuring to fix.

Why I'm changing it: if I keep going at 70/30, that 30% is what has to cover the dump fees, the fuel, the insurance, the business filings and the equipment, plus the time I spend quoting jobs and chasing customers. There's nothing left in it to put a second crew on the road. And if I can't do that, neither of us gets anywhere.

What you stop carrying

  • Self-employment tax. Right now you owe the full 15.3% yourself. As an employee you pay half and I pay the other half. On a four-day week that's roughly $4,000 a year in self-employment tax you're no longer paying — your own deductions still affect what you end up owing, so treat it as the direction, not an exact figure.
  • No more quarterly estimated payments. Tax comes out of each check.
  • No liability. If something goes wrong on a property, that's on the business, not on you.

The trailer

I want this kept separate from your wage, on purpose. It's your property and you should be paid for it as your property, not have it buried in an hourly rate.

We'll write one page covering the rate, who handles maintenance, and what happens if it gets damaged on a job. It stays yours. If you ever want out of the arrangement, you take your trailer and the job doesn't change.

Where this goes

This rate is the starting point, not the plan.

As soon as I have a real crew — two to five people working jobs in my truck with my equipment — you move straight to Director of Field Operations, and the pay moves up significantly with it.

That isn't a someday thing I'm dangling to get you to sign. It's the entire reason I'm changing how we do this. I can't build toward a crew on the old arrangement, and when the crew exists I need someone running it who already knows how we work.

At that point you're running crews rather than just working them. I'll put the number in writing when I can stand behind it — but the trigger is the crew existing, not a date and not how I'm feeling about it that month.
